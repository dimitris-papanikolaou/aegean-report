Section 2Paternal lineage

Haplogroup R-FGC4547, on the path R1a → M417 → Z645 → Z93 → Z94 → Z2124 → Z2122 → Y57 → FGC4547.

Called from 4.55 million Y-chromosome reads at ~14.9× coverage and independently confirmed by YFull, which places the clade's formation at ≈4,100 years ago and the common ancestor of its known members at ≈3,600 years ago.

A genuinely private branch

The son carries 32 Y variants not present in YFull's database, all verified directly in his own reads (100% derived, zero reference reads).

Because "absent from a commercial database" is a weak claim, the same positions were tested against nine ancient genomes spanning 4,605 BP to the 10th century CE — every published R1a-Z93 descendant with usable read-level data, from the steppe, the Carpathian Basin, Moldova and central Russia.

31 of the 32 variants were testable in at least one ancient genome, 23 of them at transversion sites — immune to the chemical damage that mimics variants in ancient DNA — and not one was derived in any sample. Each ancient sample independently reproduced its own published haplogroup from reads alone, the positive control for the method.

An incidental cross-check of the dating. Four Bronze Age men from Russia (4,504–4,605 BP) have coverage at FGC4547 and all four carry the ancestral allele — they predate the mutation. YFull's ~4,100-year estimate came from a mutation-rate calculation on modern samples; archaeological samples older than that estimate lacking the mutation is independent confirmation from a different kind of evidence.

The lineage reached Greece from the north, not through Anatolia

Autosomal models could not distinguish a steppe route from a Caucasus one (§7). The Y chromosome can, because it is a single lineage with a known distribution in the ancient record. All 20,763 ancient individuals in the reference database with an automated Y call were tabulated.

Anatolia and the Caucasus are zero in every pre-Classical period: 0/188 Mesolithic, 0/63 Neolithic, 0/139 Chalcolithic, 0/164 Bronze Age, 0/182 Late Bronze/Iron Age. On the steppe the clade appears exactly when archaeology predicts — 0% before the Chalcolithic, 1.0% Chalcolithic, 9.5% Bronze Age, 13.0% Late Bronze/Iron Age.

The zero is well-sampled, not an absence of data. With 0 observed in 554 individuals, the 95% upper bound on the clade's frequency there is 0.54%. If its true frequency had matched the steppe's 9.5%, the probability of observing none in 554 is 9.6 × 10−25. The region's lineages are well characterised — J2 (13.2%), G2 (8.1%) and J1 (5.4%) dominate — and R1 as a whole does not reach the top eight there, against 27.7% on the steppe.

The clade was in the southern Balkans in time. The two pre-Classical Z93 carriers nearest the Aegean are both from Thrace, in present-day Bulgaria, one dated 1866–1615 calBCE.

What this does not show. One uniparental lineage is one ancestor per generation. The autosomal steppe contribution is only 10–15%, and a steppe paternal line on a majority-Anatolian genome is the ordinary pattern for the post-Bronze-Age Aegean. It also does not establish descent from those Thracian individuals, who are typed at a much coarser resolution than this lineage.

≈55% Anatolian Neolithic, ≈30% Caucasus/Iranian, ≈12% steppe.

qpAdm fits the genomes as a mixture of explicitly named ancient source populations, testing the fit against a set of outgroups. ADMIXTURE is an unsupervised model that infers components from 1,995 modern and ancient individuals without being told what the sources are; the number of components was chosen by cross-validation across ten independent random starts per value, and K = 8 minimises the mean error while also being the most reproducible value tested.

The two ADMIXTURE analyses — one projecting the family onto components estimated without them, one estimating everything jointly — agree within about four percentage points and both fall inside the qpAdm ranges. Four continental control components (Native American, Sub-Saharan African, East Asian, Papuan) sit at essentially zero in both individuals, as they should.

A note on labels. The Anatolian-Neolithic component's strongest modern loadings are Sardinian and Basque. Those are the modern populations that retain the most early-farmer ancestry; this is not a claim of Sardinian or Basque descent.

Robustness. These proportions are stable across every processing choice tested — variant caller, genotype-sampling method, and random seed. The proportions are reliable; formal model-acceptance p-values on a single genome are not, and are not relied on here.

Section 6Position among modern populations

Both individuals fall within the eastern Mediterranean cluster in principal component analysis of 27,594 individuals.

Haplotype sharing: inside the Greek range, at its eastern edge

The finer question — where within Greek variation — is answered by haplotype sharing rather than by allele frequencies. The genome is broken into segments and each segment matched to the individual it most resembles among 1,311 people from 44 populations. The resulting copying profile is far more sensitive to fine-scale structure than the methods of §4.

Two corrections had to be made before the profiles meant anything. Both are worth stating, because each one uncorrected gives a confidently wrong answer.

First, raw copying shares measure panel size, not ancestry. The largest single donor for this family is Spanish at 16.1% — and Spanish is also the largest donor for Greeks (16.9%) and for almost everyone else, because 228 of the 1,311 reference individuals are Spanish. Every share must be divided by that population's share of the panel before it can be read.

Second, the automatic clustering misplaced the family entirely. The hierarchical tree put them in a Levantine group with no Greek within nine levels — contradicting everything else here. The cause is specific: the son copies 5.2% of his genome from his mother and she copies 5.7% from him. Each is the other's fifth-largest donor out of 44 populations, because no other person in the panel has a close relative present. That mutual sharing correctly identifies them as a pair and simultaneously makes the pair an outlier against every other group, so the clustering attached them wherever the residual signal fit. Their actual profiles show nothing Levantine:

The probability of 33 of 34 populations deviating in the predicted direction by chance is 2×10−9; a rank test of East against Northwest gives P = 2.5×10−6.

The finding. This family is Greek — 53 of 54 measurements fall inside the range of the 19 individual Greeks, and the copying profile is a Greek profile. Within that range they sit consistently at the eastern Aegean end: modestly more sharing with Anatolia, the Levant, the Caucasus and Crete, less with the Balkans and northern Europe. The shifts are small — roughly 10–25% in relative terms — and no single one is individually remarkable. The result is the consistency of direction across 34 populations, not the size of any one deviation.

Three limits on how far this can be pushed. Mother and son are not independent observations — a son's maternal chromosomes are his mother's, so the two identical probabilities above are one family measured twice. The comparison is to 19 specific Greek individuals, not to Greeks in general. And this family's data were produced by a different technology from the reference panel's — sequencing rather than genotyping arrays — a difference known to distort exactly this kind of comparison; see §7.

Regional origin within Greece is a plausible reading of an eastern tilt, but these data cannot separate it from other histories producing the same pattern, and no family history was used in the analysis.

Slavic ancestry

Not distinguishable from zero in formal modelling, with an upper bound of roughly 20%. Where a model includes an Aegean source population the fitted Slavic contribution collapses to 5–10% with standard errors spanning zero; where no Aegean source is available, a Slavic proxy absorbs unrelated variance and the apparent contribution rises. The haplotype analysis above points the same way from an independent direction — northwestern European and Balkan sharing is consistently below the Greek average, not above it.

A comparison this report does not make

An earlier draft placed the family against 42 modern populations using the method of §4, and reported them displaced toward West Asia relative to Greeks. That comparison has been withdrawn. The model fits none of the 42 populations, and a second version of it — adding the ancestral source that the failures identified as missing — reverses the direction of the result while also failing. Two models that disagree about the sign of an effect, both rejected by the data, cannot support a claim in either direction. The finding above rests on haplotype sharing instead, which is a different kind of evidence and does not depend on a fitted model.

The gradient across those 42 populations remains a valid description of how these ancestry components vary geographically; it is the family's specific position within it that the method could not establish.

Section 7What these data cannot determine

Reporting these clearly is part of the result.

The father's genetic contribution cannot be isolated

The original aim included characterising the deceased father by subtracting the mother's contribution from the son's genome. This is not achievable with these two samples, for a reason that is technical rather than biological.

The two genomes were sequenced on different platforms — the son on MGI/Complete Genomics DNBSEQ, the mother on Illumina. This produces small systematic differences in genotype calls that are larger than the biological signal being sought.

The decisive test: the son's maternally-inherited alleles are his mother's DNA, so a statistic comparing them must be zero. It is not.

The comparison that should be zero is consistently larger than the comparison that contains the father, and all 28 reference populations deviate in the same direction — a pattern ancestry cannot produce, since it would require the same bias relative to Sardinians, Saudis, Lithuanians and Chechens alike.

Every available remedy was tested. Re-aligning the reads removed only ~4% of the effect. Filtering to well-behaved sequence contexts did not remove it. Re-calling both genomes with an independent variant caller did not remove it — even though that caller eliminated ~91% of the underlying allelic imbalance, confirming the problem is the sequencing chemistry itself and not the software applied to it.

The only route to this question is a new sample — ideally the father's sister, sequenced on the same platform as the mother, allowing direct observation of the paternal side rather than reconstruction by subtraction.

Why the haplotype result in §6 is nonetheless reported. The same platform difference applies there, and more sharply, because haplotype matching is sensitive to sequencing and to which positions were measured. Two things argue the eastern tilt is not an artefact of it. The measure used divides out each population's share of the reference panel, which is the obvious channel through which a technical difference would act — it would make the family appear to copy more from whichever panels are largest or best measured, and that effect is removed by construction. And a technical artefact has no reason to organise itself geographically: it would flatten the profile or tilt it toward large panels, not place 17 of 17 eastern Mediterranean populations above the Greek average and 17 of 17 northern European ones below it. The result is reported at moderate confidence for that reason, not high, and it is the one conclusion here we would most want independently checked.

Whether the steppe influx was male-biased cannot be determined

Whole communities moving, and men arriving to father children with local women, leave the same autosomal signature but different X-chromosome signatures. Women carry two X chromosomes and men one, so two thirds of the X copies in a population descend from female ancestors against one half of autosomal copies; an ancestry component contributed mainly by men is therefore under-represented on the X. Steppe expansion into central and northern Europe is reported as strongly male-biased, so the same was expected here.

The test was run — the same model fitted twice, once to the autosomes and once to the X, with the pre-steppe Cretan population as a control that had to come back flat. The control passed cleanly (Z = 0.12). The result is that the test has no power to answer the question.

The X chromosome carries 49,581 of the analysed positions against roughly 1.18 million on the autosomes, and after requiring every population to be genotyped the X analysis rests on 15,545 usable positions against 677,066. Standard errors are 5.6 times larger. The smallest difference such a test could detect is 0.230. Under a completely male-mediated influx — every steppe ancestor a man — the expected difference for these populations would be between 0.035 and 0.092.

The detection threshold is 2.5 times larger than the largest effect a 100%-male influx could produce. No target reached significance, and only three of eight even pointed in the predicted direction, but neither fact carries information: an instrument that cannot see a maximal effect tells you nothing when it sees nothing. This is reported as an unanswered question, not as evidence of no sex bias. Answering it would need whole-genome sequence data from the ancient individuals rather than the targeted panel available.

The admixture cannot be dated from these genomes directly

Methods that date admixture from the decay of ancestry along chromosomes have a resolution limit of roughly 60 generations under these conditions. The event of interest is roughly 130 generations old, placing it beyond reach. Three such analyses were attempted and none produced a usable signal; the timing in §5 comes from dated ancient remains instead, which is the more reliable route regardless.

The route question is answered only for the paternal line

Autosomal models cannot distinguish a steppe route from a Caucasus/Armenian one: the two fit comparably, and which is preferred changes with the choice of reference population. This reflects genuine statistical non-identifiability, not a limitation of effort. The Y-chromosome evidence in §2 answers the question for the paternal lineage only.

Statistical practice

Two conventions were adopted after they proved to matter.

Replicate random seeds. Reducing a diploid genome to one allele per position involves a random choice. Repeating an identical analysis with different random seeds moved model p-values as much as 15-fold, while the ancestry proportions moved only slightly. Proportions are therefore reported and p-values are not relied upon.

Pre-registered expectations and negative controls. Where a published result could be reproduced as a check, the expected outcome was written down before the analysis was run. Where an analysis could produce a spurious positive, a group expected to show no signal was analysed alongside. One dating analysis produced five mutually consistent, statistically strong results that were rejected on the strength of such a control — without it, those results would have looked entirely sound.

Section 9Interpretation

The genetic evidence describes a family whose ancestry is characteristic of the Aegean since the Bronze Age: predominantly descended from the Neolithic farming populations of Anatolia who settled Greece from roughly 6500 BCE, with a substantial Caucasus/Iranian-related component, and a minority steppe-related component that entered the region around 2000–1900 BCE — the period associated with the emergence of Mycenaean Greece.

Measured in the same analysis as the ancient remains themselves, the family's steppe proportion is indistinguishable from Late Bronze Age Crete and Late Bronze Age Greece — placing them within the range of Mycenaean-era populations rather than requiring any later population movement. Consistently, no detectable Slavic contribution was found, and the fine-scale haplotype evidence places the family at the eastern Aegean end of Greek variation.

Two questions asked of these data could not be answered by them, and are reported as such rather than resolved: the father's individual contribution, blocked by a sequencing-platform artefact that survived every remedy tested, and whether the steppe influx was male-biased, for which the available X-chromosome data are too sparse by a factor of 2.5 to detect even a maximal effect.

The paternal line is a distinct thread within that picture: it descends from Bronze Age steppe populations, reached the southern Balkans by the Middle Bronze Age, and has since accumulated 32 variants found in no other tested genome, ancient or modern.

The maternal line now has an origin

The paternal line and the Dodecanese

Section 2 reported that no Greek, Anatolian or Balkan sample appears anywhere in this paternal lineage's region of the tree. A 2026 study of Deep Mani reports the only other published Greek R1a-Z93 lineage, and notes Y-STR matches for it in Karpathos and Rhodes — adjacent to Kasos, and the paternal line may be from Rhodes itself.

This is a geographic coincidence, not a match. That lineage sits on a branch which separated from this family's roughly 4,500 years ago, so they are not close paternal relatives and those STR matches belong to their branch. What it supports is a target for future sampling — the Dodecanese may hold more than one R1a-Z93 lineage — rather than a result.

What the history could not deliver

An Armenian great-grandmother on the maternal line implies the mother carries ~25% Armenian-related ancestry and her son ~12.5%, so she should carry about twice his. That is falsifiable with a number attached. It could not be measured, in five separate attempts.

A test against the distribution of 32 populations passed its own controls and found nothing — the standard errors exceed the entire spread of between-population variation. A three-source model matched to the documented regions collapsed, with weights running past ±200. A two-source retreat assigned mainland Greeks −55% Armenian ancestry and Cretan Greeks +37%, failing its controls outright. An attempt to recover the father by subtracting the mother from the son produced point estimates that matched the pedigree well but dissolved under a chromosome-drop jackknife. And a comparison against the only Anatolian-Greek reference samples in existence put the family as different from three of the four, on sample sizes too small to settle it either way.

The reason is consistent across all five and is not a matter of effort: Armenians and Aegean Greeks are both mixtures of Anatolian farmers and Caucasus hunter-gatherers, so distinguishing 25% Armenian from 0% in an Aegean background means resolving a small shift in the ratio of two deep components that both populations already carry. The methods used here cannot do that.

So the Armenian ancestry is established by the mitochondrial evidence, and its proportion is not measurable with these data — a division of labour between two kinds of evidence rather than a gap in the result.

One limit the disclosure exposed

There is no Cappadocian, Karamanlı or central Anatolian Greek reference sample in any panel used in this report. The mother's ancestral population is unsampled. Part of every “differs from population X” statement above is therefore the reference panel's gap rather than her ancestry being unusual — a caveat that could not have been identified without knowing where to look. A related one surfaced with it: the two modern Greek panels in the data differ from each other (p = 0.001), so “Greek” is not a single stable reference either.

And one question stays open. Relative measures cannot distinguish “more eastern ancestry” from “less northern ancestry”, because the shares sum to one, and the model that could separate them returns inadmissible weights. The family's position is established; its decomposition into the presence of one thing versus the absence of another is not.

What your DNA shows

A companion to the full report. The same findings, in ordinary English.

What we set out to do

Two whole genomes were read from start to finish — a mother's and her adult son's. A genome is the complete set of DNA instructions a person carries, about three billion chemical letters long. Reading all of it, rather than the small sample that consumer ancestry kits look at, makes it possible to ask deeper questions: not "which country are my relatives from" but "which ancient populations did my ancestors belong to, and when did they arrive where they arrived."

The first thing the data confirmed was the obvious thing. The two samples are genetically a mother and her son, exactly as expected — the DNA sharing between them is textbook. Neither genome shows signs of cousin marriage anywhere in the recent family tree. These are dull results, but they are the checks that let the interesting results be trusted.

The deep ancestry: three ancient populations

Most of Greek ancestry, and yours, traces back to a small number of ancient populations that mixed thousands of years ago. Your genomes break down roughly as follows.

About 55% comes from Anatolian Neolithic farmers. These were the people who invented farming in what is now Turkey and the Near East, and who spread into Greece from roughly 6500 BCE, bringing wheat, sheep and villages with them. They are the deepest and largest layer of your ancestry.

About 30% comes from a Caucasus and Iranian-related population — ancient hunter-gatherers and their descendants from the mountains between the Black Sea and the Caspian.

About 12% comes from the Eurasian steppe — the grasslands north of the Black Sea, home to mobile herding peoples of the Bronze Age.

Please read those percentages as approximate. Three completely different statistical methods were used to work them out, methods that share almost none of their assumptions, and all three landed in the same place — which is why these numbers are among the firmest things in the report. But each method gives a range rather than a single figure, and the ranges are a few percentage points wide. "Roughly 55, 30 and 12" is the honest way to say it.

One thing worth being clear about. "55% Anatolian farmer" does not mean 55% Turkish. These labels name populations that lived seven to nine thousand years ago, long before any modern nation, language or border existed. Modern Turks, modern Greeks and modern Italians all carry ancestry from those same farmers in differing amounts. The same caution applies to a curiosity in the analysis: the modern populations that best represent the "Anatolian farmer" component happen to be Sardinians and Basques, simply because those groups have held onto more early-farmer ancestry than anyone else. It is not a claim of Sardinian or Basque relatives.

When the steppe ancestry arrived: around 2000–1900 BCE

The steppe component is the smallest slice, and the most historically loaded one, because its arrival in Greece coincides with the emergence of Mycenaean civilisation — the world of Agamemnon, Linear B tablets and shaft graves.

The date here does not come from any clock inside your DNA. It comes from skeletons. Every ancient Greek and Aegean population with usable DNA and a radiocarbon date was tested with the same method used on your genomes, and asked a simple question: does this population need steppe ancestry to explain it, or not?

The answer splits cleanly in time. Every population from the Neolithic through the early Middle Bronze Age — six of them, spanning roughly 6200 to 2000 BCE — shows no steppe ancestry at all. Every population from about 2000 BCE onward — eight of them, including the people buried at the Palace of Nestor and across Crete, Attica and the Peloponnese — requires it. Not one population sits on the wrong side of that line. And helpfully, the two populations either side of the boundary are both Cretan, so the pattern is tracking time rather than geography.

Your family was then measured inside that same analysis — the same model, the same genetic markers, the same computer run — rather than measured separately and compared afterwards. That matters, because it removes the possibility that a difference in method is doing the work. Your steppe proportion came out at 0.104 for the son and 0.120 for the mother, with an uncertainty of about 0.035 either way. Late Bronze Age Crete is 0.106; Late Bronze Age Greece generally is 0.105.

Those numbers are statistically indistinguishable. You carry the same proportion of steppe ancestry as the Mycenaean-era dead, measured on the same ruler. This is the strongest continuity result in the whole report. It is a consistency rather than a pedigree — it does not name an ancestor — but it is a real one.

This part of the analysis also served as a test of the whole pipeline. The expected answer, from published research on Minoan and Mycenaean remains, was written down before the analysis was run. It came out matching. That is reassurance that the machinery works, not a result bent to fit.

The father's line: from the steppe, through the Balkans

Everyone inherits a Y chromosome from their father, essentially unchanged, so it traces one single thread — father to father to father — back through time. The family of related Y chromosomes a man belongs to is called a haplogroup, and it works rather like a surname that mutates very slowly: every few generations a small typing error appears, and that error is passed down to all descendants, marking out a branch of the tree.

Your paternal haplogroup is called R-FGC4547, a branch of a large family known as R1a-Z93 that formed roughly 4,100 years ago.

The branch is genuinely your own. The son carries 32 mutations on his Y chromosome that appear in no other genome yet tested — not in the large commercial databases of living men, and not in ancient skeletons. Because "absent from a database" is a weak claim on its own, all 32 positions were checked directly against nine ancient men from the steppe, Hungary, Moldova and Russia — every published relative of this lineage whose data was good enough to test. Not one of them carried a single one of the 32. This is a private branch of the human family tree, and it is yours.

There was an unexpected bonus in that check. Four Bronze Age men from Russia, around 4,500 years old, turn out to be just slightly too early: they lack the defining mutation of your branch entirely. The estimated age of the branch, about 4,100 years, was calculated from mutation rates in living men — a completely different kind of evidence. Having archaeological skeletons that are older than that estimate and correspondingly lack the mutation is an independent confirmation of the date.

And the route can be established. This matters because there are two plausible ways steppe ancestry could reach Greece: south through the Caucasus and across Anatolia, or west across the steppe and down through the Balkans. Your overall genome cannot tell these apart — both fit the data about equally well, and which one looks better depends on arbitrary choices in the analysis. That is a genuine dead end, not a lack of effort.

The Y chromosome can tell them apart, because it is a single lineage whose distribution in the ancient record is known. Of 554 ancient men from Anatolia and the Caucasus dating to the Bronze Age or earlier, not one belongs to R1a-Z93 — zero in every single period, from the Mesolithic through the Iron Age. On the steppe, by contrast, the lineage is present in about 3% of men overall, rising to roughly one man in ten during the Bronze Age, appearing precisely when archaeology says it should. That Anatolian zero is not an absence of data; 554 men is plenty, and the region's actual lineages are well known and different. Meanwhile the two nearest ancient carriers of this lineage to the Aegean are both from Thrace, in today's Bulgaria — one of them dated to roughly 1866–1615 BCE, which puts the lineage in the southern Balkans at the right moment.

So: the paternal line came down from the steppe via the Balkans, not through Anatolia. This is one of the firmest findings in the report.

Two honest qualifications. A single paternal line represents just one ancestor per generation out of many, and a steppe paternal line sitting atop a mostly-Anatolian genome is the ordinary pattern for the Aegean after the Bronze Age — this is not a rewriting of the overall ancestry. And it does not mean descent from those particular Thracian men, whose DNA was read at a much coarser resolution than yours.

The mother's line

Mitochondrial DNA is a tiny separate loop of DNA passed only from mother to child, so it traces the maternal thread the way the Y traces the paternal one. Yours is haplogroup K1a19, and mother and son match at every one of its 16,569 positions — as they must, and a further confirmation of the relationship.

The closest ancient relatives found differ by a single letter, and they come from Armenia, Roman-era Turkey and late-antique Croatia. That spread — Caucasus, Anatolia, the Adriatic — fits a lineage distributed around the eastern Mediterranean in Classical antiquity. But three skeletons cannot pin down where it started, and it would be overreaching to claim otherwise.

The maternal line cannot be dated. Dating a lineage depends on counting up mutations unique to it, and this mitochondrial genome carries none outside the handful of positions that mutate so often they are useless for the purpose. There is simply no clock to read here. This is a genuine blank, not a number awaiting refinement.

Where you sit among modern Greeks

Both of you fall comfortably within the eastern Mediterranean cluster when compared against tens of thousands of people worldwide, and within the range of Greek variation specifically. The finer question is where inside that range — and answering it needed a different, more sensitive method than the one behind the percentages above.

That method works on long stretches rather than single positions. Your genome is cut into segments, and each segment is matched to whichever of 1,311 reference people from 44 populations it most closely resembles. The tally of who you resemble, and where, is a much finer instrument than an overall ancestry percentage.

The answer: you are Greek, and you sit at the eastern edge of Greek. Of 54 separate measurements, 53 fall inside the range of the 19 individual Greeks you were compared against — your profile is a Greek profile. Within that range, the position is consistently directional: modestly more sharing with Anatolia, the Levant, the Caucasus and Crete, and modestly less with the Balkans and northern Europe. Of 34 populations tested, 33 lean that same way. The odds of that happening by chance are about two in a billion.

Two things keep this at "likely" rather than "settled". The shifts are small — roughly 10 to 25% more or less sharing than the Greek average, not dramatic differences. And you and your mother are not two independent pieces of evidence: half of a son's DNA is his mother's, so this is one family measured twice, not two families agreeing. It is also a comparison against 19 specific Greek people rather than against Greeks in general.

Relatedly, formal modelling finds no detectable Slavic ancestry — the estimate cannot be distinguished from zero, with an upper limit around 20%. The haplotype work points the same way from a different direction: your sharing with Balkan and northeastern European populations is consistently below the Greek average, not above it.

Two ways this analysis first went wrong

Both are worth telling, because each one, left uncorrected, produced a confident and completely wrong answer.

The automatic grouping step put your family in the Levant. The software that sorts everyone into related clusters placed you in a group of Druze, Palestinians and Iranians, with no Greek anywhere near you. Taken at face value it contradicted everything else in this report.

The cause turned out to be endearing. The son copies 5.2% of his genome from his mother, and she copies 5.7% from him. Each of you is the other's fifth-largest "donor" out of 44 populations — because you are the only close relatives anywhere in a reference panel of 1,311 people. That mutual resemblance is entirely correct; it is the algorithm noticing you are mother and son. But it also makes the two of you, as a pair, look unlike every other group in the panel, so the sorting step parked you wherever the leftover signal happened to fit. Your actual profiles are unremarkable: about 14% of your genome matches Levantine people, which is simply the normal eastern Mediterranean background — Greeks show 13.5%, Turks 14.7%, southern Italians 14.3%. An actually Levantine group, the Druze, shows 48.8%.

The raw numbers said your biggest genetic affinity was Spanish. At 16.1%, Spanish was your largest single match. It is also the largest single match for Greeks (16.9%) and for almost everybody else — because 228 of the 1,311 reference people are Spanish. You resemble Spaniards most simply because there are more Spaniards to resemble. Every figure had to be divided by how many people each population contributed before it meant anything at all.

A comparison the report no longer makes

An earlier version of this report said your family was shifted toward West Asia compared with Greeks, based on the percentage-style method rather than the segment-matching one. That claim has been withdrawn.

The reason is worth understanding, because it is the kind of thing that separates a result from a number. That model turned out not to fit any of the 42 modern populations it was tested on — including Greeks — which meant it was the wrong description of how those populations formed. A corrected version, adding an ancestral population the failures showed was missing, fit no better and reversed the direction of the answer. Two versions of a model that disagree about which way an effect points, both rejected by the data, cannot support a conclusion either way. So the claim was dropped rather than kept with a caveat, and the eastern-edge finding above rests on the segment-matching evidence instead — a different kind of evidence, better suited to placing one family inside one country's variation, and not dependent on a model fitting.

What could not be answered

This is as much a part of the result as the findings, and it should not be dressed up.

The father's genetic contribution could not be isolated

The original hope was to reconstruct something about the deceased father by comparing mother and son and, in effect, subtracting her out — whatever the son has that his mother does not have must have come from his father. This did not work. Not partly, not approximately: it failed.

The reason is technical rather than biological. The two genomes were read on different brands of sequencing machine — the son's on one manufacturer's system, the mother's on another's. Each machine makes its own small, systematic reading errors, and the difference between the two machines turned out to be larger than the biological signal being looked for.

There is a decisive way to see this. The half of the son's DNA that he inherited from his mother simply is his mother's DNA. A measurement comparing those two things must come out at zero, because there is no biology in it at all. It did not come out at zero — and worse, it came out consistently larger than the measurement that actually contained the father's contribution. The signal being sought was smaller than the noise sitting on top of it.

Every available remedy was tried. Re-processing the raw data from scratch removed about 4% of the problem. Filtering down to the best-behaved stretches of the genome did not remove it. Re-reading both genomes with entirely different analysis software did not remove it either — even though that software fixed most of the underlying irregularity, which confirms the problem lies in the sequencing chemistry itself, not in any software choice. There is no clever correction waiting to be found.

What would answer it: the father's sister. Sequencing the aunt would allow the paternal side to be observed directly rather than reconstructed by subtraction. Crucially, her sample would need to be sequenced on the same platform as the mother's — otherwise the identical problem reappears in a new form. That is the one clear route forward from here.

Whether the steppe arrivals were mostly men could not be determined

And here, "we found nothing" genuinely means nothing. This is the clearest example in the report of why that distinction matters.

Whole families migrating into Greece, and mostly men arriving and having children with local women, leave the same trace in the bulk of the genome. They differ on the X chromosome: women carry two X chromosomes and men one, so the X records a different mix of male and female ancestors than the rest of the genome does. An ancestry brought in mainly by men shows up thinner there. Elsewhere in Europe the steppe expansion is reported as strongly male-dominated, so the same was expected here.

The test was run properly, including a check on a pre-steppe Cretan population that had to come back flat, and did. The finding is that the test cannot see an answer either way. The X chromosome holds only 49,581 of the DNA positions measured here, against about 1.18 million on the rest of the genome — and once every population has to be reliably measured at a position, just 15,545 remain usable.

The consequence is arithmetic. The smallest difference such a test could detect is 0.230. Yet even if every single steppe ancestor had been a man, the difference produced would be only 0.035 to 0.092. The ruler is 2.5 times too coarse to measure even the largest effect the question could possibly produce.

So nothing came out significant, and that tells us nothing at all. It is not evidence that families migrated rather than men. "We looked and there is nothing there" and "our instrument is not fine enough to see this" are entirely different statements, and this is firmly the second. Settling it would need far richer DNA from the ancient skeletons than exists today.

The mixing itself cannot be dated from your genomes. There are methods that date ancient mixing events by measuring how thoroughly the different ancestries have been shuffled along the chromosomes, but they reach back only about 60 generations. The event of interest is around 130 generations ago — twice as far as the method can see. Three attempts produced nothing usable. The date of 2000–1900 BCE comes from radiocarbon-dated skeletons instead, which is the better route anyway.

The route question is answered for the paternal line only. As noted above, the overall genome cannot distinguish a steppe route from a Caucasus one. Only the Y chromosome settles it, and it settles it for that one thread.

Afterwards: what happened when you told us where the family came from

Everything above was finished and written up before anyone doing the analysis knew a single place name from your family history. That ordering is the whole point of this section. Nothing was adjusted to land on a known answer, because the answer was not known; no method was chosen, and no borderline result kept or dropped, with the documented origins in view.

The same ordering also sets a limit on what may be claimed. None of this was a prediction. Nobody wrote down “Kasos” or “Cappadocia” in advance and then checked. What follows are consistency checks: results computed blind, held up afterwards against the documented history to see whether the two accounts sit comfortably together. They do, in several independent places — but “consistent with” is the strongest phrase that belongs here.

The documented origins. The mother's family were Greeks of Niğde, in Cappadocia, central Anatolia. Her mother's father came from Andros, in the Cyclades. Her mother's mother was Armenian, and stands on the unbroken mother-to-daughter line. Her father's mother came from Lesbos, in the eastern Aegean. The father's family came from Kasos, in the Dodecanese, with the direct father-to-son line possibly from Rhodes. Five regions; four of them Aegean islands or Anatolia; and not one mainland Greek or Balkan line anywhere.

Where the blind results and the history agree

  • No northern medieval ancestry. Modelling gives mainland Greeks about 28% early-medieval northern ancestry. Your figures come out at essentially nothing for the son and much lower than mainland Greeks for the mother. The history has no mainland line to supply it.
  • Statistically distinguishable from mainland Greeks — and, importantly, this held when the test was repeated against a completely separate collection of 17 Greek individuals, so it is not an artefact of one reference group.
  • Of every modern population tested, the only one you could not be told apart from was Cretan Greeks. Kasos lies about 50 km from Crete.
  • Indistinguishable from a Byzantine-era burial on Chios — while mainland Greeks can be told apart from that same burial. Lesbos is the next island north of Chios.
  • Clearly different from modern Turkish samples, which is exactly what one expects of Cappadocian Greeks rather than of Turks.
  • The eastern-Aegean tilt in the copying profiles, and the raised Caucasus/Iranian-related share, both point the same way as a history made of central Anatolia, the eastern Aegean, the Cyclades and the Dodecanese.

The maternal line finally has a home

The mother-to-child line was the weakest result in the whole report. The mitochondrial genome — the small separate loop of DNA passed down only through mothers — belongs to a group called K1a19, and it carries no mutations of its own. That meant no clock to date it by and no real geography, only a label.

Then: the mother's mother's mother was Armenian, and she sits on that unbroken line. So this exact mitochondrial genome came down directly from her. K1a19 is that Armenian lineage in this family, and it is documented rather than inferred.

Two entirely independent things, neither of which knew the history, point the same way. The three closest ancient genomes ever found differ from yours by just one letter in 16,569 — and two of the three are from Armenia, 2,225 years ago, and Roman-era Anatolia, 1,900 years ago. Separately, a search of the world's public genetic database turns up only three complete K1a19 genomes in existence, and no scientific paper even names the group; two of the three come from Armenian studies, one of them from Erzurum, in the historic Armenian highlands.

Two caveats stay attached, and they matter. The third close ancient match is from Croatia, equally close, and does not fit an Armenian-only story — this lineage was spread across a wide zone from West Asia into the Balkans, with its oldest known carriers in Neolithic Iran and Chalcolithic Bulgaria. And “two out of three” is not a frequency: with three sequences in the world, it partly reflects which populations researchers happen to have sequenced, and these groups have sequenced the Caucasus heavily.

The paternal line and Rhodes: a coincidence worth noting, not a relative

A 2026 study reports the only other published Greek paternal lineage from the same broad family as yours, and notes close marker matches for it in Karpathos and Rhodes — the islands immediately beside Kasos.

That is striking, and it is worth being precise about why it is not a family match. Two men can share a rare lineage recently, meaning a common ancestor within living memory or a few centuries. That is not this. Your line and that one separated roughly 4,500 years ago — as distant as two branches of that family can be. The marker matches in Karpathos and Rhodes belong to their branch, not yours. What we have is two different, long-separated branches of a rare lineage both turning up in the same small island group. That points to where someone should sample next — the Dodecanese may hold more than one branch of this lineage — rather than to a relative.

Five things the history could not deliver

An Armenian great-grandmother on the maternal line implies a specific, checkable number: the mother should carry about 25% Armenian-related ancestry and the son about 12.5%, so roughly twice his. Five separate attempts failed to measure it. They are reported because the way they failed is informative.

One test compared your genomes against the spread of 32 populations; it passed its own quality controls and simply found nothing, because its margin of error was wider than the entire range of variation between those populations. A model built from three source populations matched to the documented regions collapsed, producing nonsense — mixture weights running past plus or minus 200, where anything outside 0 to 100% is meaningless. A simpler two-source version handed mainland Greeks minus 55% Armenian ancestry and Cretan Greeks plus 37%; a negative share of ancestry cannot exist, so that model failed outright. And the attempt to reconstruct the father by subtracting the mother from the son gave numbers that looked pleasingly close to the pedigree, then dissolved once proper error bars were put on them by re-running the calculation with each chromosome left out in turn.

The reason is the same every time, and it is not a matter of effort. Armenians and Aegean Greeks are both mixtures of the same two ancient populations — Anatolian farmers and Caucasus hunter-gatherers — just in slightly different proportions. So telling 25% Armenian from 0% Armenian against a Greek background means resolving a small shift in the ratio of two ingredients both groups already contain. These methods cannot see a shift that fine.

The honest statement is therefore split in two: the Armenian line is established, by the mitochondrial evidence and the documented pedigree together, and its size is simply not measurable from these data. That is a division of labour between two kinds of evidence, not a hole in the result.

One limit the disclosure exposed

Knowing where to look revealed something no amount of analysis would have found on its own: there is no Cappadocian, Karamanlı or central Anatolian Greek reference sample in any database used in this report. The mother's actual ancestral population has never been sampled by anyone. So part of every “differs from population X” statement above reflects a gap in the world's reference collections, not her ancestry being unusual. A related point surfaced alongside it: the two modern Greek reference panels in the data differ measurably from each other, so even “Greek” is not one fixed yardstick.

One question also stays open. Because these shares must add up to 100%, “more eastern ancestry” and “less northern ancestry” cannot be told apart by relative measures, and the model that could separate them returns impossible weights. Your position among populations is established; whether it comes from the presence of one thing or the absence of another is not.

In short

Your ancestry is characteristic of the Aegean since the Bronze Age: mostly descended from the Neolithic farmers who brought agriculture from Anatolia into Greece around 6500 BCE, with a substantial Caucasus/Iranian-related layer, and a minority steppe component that arrived around 2000–1900 BCE, at the dawn of Mycenaean Greece — in proportions statistically indistinguishable from the Mycenaean-era dead themselves, measured inside the same analysis rather than compared across two. Within Greek variation you sit at its eastern Aegean edge. No Slavic ancestry is detectable. The maternal line runs back into the eastern Mediterranean of Classical antiquity, undatable with present data. And the paternal line is a thread of its own: down from the Bronze Age steppe, through the Balkans, into Greece — carrying 32 mutations that, so far as anyone can currently tell, belong to no one else alive or dead.
